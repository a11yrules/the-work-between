### Episode overview
This solo episode is about urban sketching, disability, and the assumptions hidden inside familiar advice about how artists should learn.

Sketch from life. Draw directly in ink. Don’t use photographs. Don’t start with pencil. There are good reasons for that advice, but it becomes a problem when it turns into a prescription for everybody. Learning on a busy street asks more of some bodies and brains before the pen even touches the page.

I talk about finding somewhere to put my wheelchair, standing with a cane, dealing with pain, fatigue, weather, noise, movement, and the feeling of being watched while I’m still trying to understand a roof line. Each one takes energy and attention away from the drawing.

Photographs and pencils give me room to work on perspective and proportion without doing everything at once. They can act as scaffolding while I learn. Other things are accommodations I may always need. Becoming a better artist won’t make an inaccessible street corner accessible.

I still want to draw on location. Being there changes what I notice and the choices I make. I just need a way of learning, and a place to draw, that accounts for the body and brain I actually have.

### In this episode

- When useful artistic traditions become rigid prescriptions
- What urban sketching can demand of disabled bodies before the drawing begins
- Finding a usable view when you use a wheelchair or cane
- How pain, fatigue, temperature, noise, and movement compete for attention
- Trying to learn while feeling watched in public
- Using photographs to study perspective at my own pace
- Pencil and photography as scaffolding for learning
- The difference between scaffolding and accommodation
- Why artistic skill does not eliminate access needs
- Drawing on location without pretending every sketcher has the same body or brain
- What I value about the Urban Sketchers manifesto

### A quote that stayed with me

> "I may get to a point where I no longer need a photograph to understand a roofline, but I’m not going to practice my way out of being a wheelchair user."
